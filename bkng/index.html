Feature

Increasing Conversion Through TPI Payment Failure Recovery

Role UX Designer
Company Booking.com
Timeline 4 months, 2019
Team 1 UX Copywriter, 1 PM, 1 Full-Stack Dev, 1 Mobile Dev, 1 Data Analyst

The final shipped design. A structured comparison table on both platforms that gave users a clear path forward without leaving the booking flow.

TL;DR
What

A recovery flow that presents a comparable Booking.com room immediately after a third-party payment fails, turning a dead end into a decision point.

Problem

TPI rooms had structural payment limitations. When payment failed, users hit a generic error with no next step. Between 18% and 33% abandoned the platform entirely.

My Role

Spotted the opportunity through my own analysis of payment funnel data, brought it to the PM as a product pitch, and drove the full design process: flow design, four rounds of prototype testing on UserTesting.com, copy direction, and final specs.

Decision

Chose to surface a comparable Booking.com native room immediately after the failure rather than redesigning the error message or attempting to fix the payment limitation. The limitation was contractual and unfixable. The real opportunity was in the conversion, not the error state.

Impact

Positive net conversion uplift on both app and web. Drop-off after failed payments decreased significantly. High engagement on the comparison module confirmed users were reading and acting on the alternative, not dismissing it.

Context

A strategic bet on third-party inventory that created an unowned failure mode

Booking.com offered third-party inventory (TPI) rooms sourced from providers like Agoda alongside its own native inventory. The commercial logic was clear: TPI rooms were cheaper, and lower prices in search results drove more users into the booking funnel.

The trade-offs were disclosed upfront: TPI rooms required advance payment, were non-refundable, had limited payment method support, and came with reduced customer service. Users who understood those conditions converted well. But this also meant Booking.com had a structural dependency on third-party systems at the most critical moment: payment.

Limited payment method support was not a technical edge case. It was built into the TPI model. That made payment failures a predictable, recurring event, and the absence of a recovery path had been quietly accepted as an expected cost of the business model. I came across the post-failure abandonment data while reviewing payment funnel metrics. Nobody had scoped it as a problem to solve. I decided to.

The Problem

Known, measurable, and treated as acceptable, until it wasn't

The existing error state was a modal with a single message: "We're sorry, we have not been able to take your payment." One button: OK. No explanation, no alternatives, no next step. From the user's perspective, the booking was over.

Analytics confirmed the scale. Between 18% and 33% of users who hit a TPI payment failure abandoned the platform entirely. At the volume of bookings Booking.com processed, this was a significant and measurable revenue leak, not an edge case.

The original experience on mobile. A user who had already selected their hotel, entered their details, and provided payment information was met with a dead end and no recovery path.

0 recovery options in the original error state
4 mo. from pitch to full launch on mobile and web

After the PM agreed to pursue it, I defined the scope myself. The obvious path was to focus narrowly on the error state: better copy, a retry button, something contained. But Booking.com already had native inventory with pay-at-property as a standard feature. The recovery was not about fixing an error message. It was about turning a structural failure into a conversion moment by connecting users to an alternative they could actually book.

The Decision

I had surfaced the problem. The harder question was what to actually build.

Once the PM agreed to pursue this, the first thing I established was the constraint boundary: what was actually fixable versus what wasn't. TPI payment limitations were contractual and technical, set by the third-party providers. We could not fix the root cause. That constraint ruled out an entire category of solutions and pointed to a single question: what could we do within the failure state itself?

I mapped two directions. Improve the error message and add a payment retry path — safer, lower lift, clearly in scope. Or use the failure moment as a conversion opportunity and surface a comparable Booking.com native room with pay-at-property immediately after the failure. I argued for the second. Booking.com already had the inventory. The structural advantage was there. The problem was that nobody had built a path to it from the failure state.

The risk of the second option was real: presenting a native room immediately after a TPI failure could damage future TPI conversion if users associated TPI with unreliability. The design had to frame the alternative as a helpful next step, not as a verdict on TPI. That constraint shaped every iteration. I used Jobs to Be Done to anchor the team: when my payment fails, I want a clear and trustworthy alternative so I do not have to start over and risk losing my booking.

I mapped the current flow against the proposed recovery flow to get cross-functional agreement before designing. The before state was a linear dead end. The after state introduced a branch at the failure point: offer a comparable Booking.com room with pay-at-property.

Left: the original flow. A linear path with no recovery. Right: the proposed flow. The failure becomes a decision point with two clear paths forward.

With the flow direction agreed, I moved into prototyping. I used UserTesting.com to test each iteration with real users, keeping cycles short. One pattern was consistent across all sessions: users in a payment failure state were already frustrated. Any added cognitive load made things significantly worse.

Design Process

Four iterations, each one informed by a specific failure in the previous

The first mobile prototype followed the existing room options design pattern: a side-by-side comparison with color-coded differences. Users found it overwhelming. Several did not register it as a comparison at all. The cognitive cost of processing two options at once, in a frustrated state, was too high.

The first structural improvement: replacing the dead-end modal with an inline recovery message that surfaced an alternative room directly in context, without navigating away.

Iteration two stripped the color cues and simplified the information hierarchy. Iteration three reduced the information to essential differences only. Both tested better, but two concerns surfaced consistently. Users could not confirm both rooms were from the same hotel. And they wanted to see a photo before making any decision.

Iterations two and three on mobile. Reducing information helped, but users still asked "are these the same hotel?" and "can I see the room?" until those two things were addressed directly.

The headline "Your payment didn't work" was consistently triggering early exits. Users read it as a permanent failure, not as an invitation to act. Changing it to signal availability rather than failure ("Alternative room available") changed user engagement with everything that followed. This was not a copy fix. It was a reframe of the entire interaction contract.

The final mobile recovery flow. Hotel name at the top, room photo, key conditions, and two clear CTAs. Shipped version.

Desktop required a different negotiation

The first desktop version used two split columns with green highlights to show differences, borrowed directly from the existing room selection pattern. Like the early mobile work, it required too much scanning and created the same hotel ambiguity problem.

Desktop iterations one and two. Moving from split cards to a unified container resolved the "are these from the same hotel?" confusion immediately.

The final version used a table pattern: left-aligned labels, horizontal dividers, two-column comparison. This deviated from the room selection team's established design pattern, which required explicit negotiation. Their pattern had been tested and optimized for browsing. But this was a different emotional context: users were not browsing, they were recovering. Fewer options, less information, one clear recommendation.

The final desktop design. Hotel name at the top, structured table for fast scanning, and two CTAs: "Book our suggestion" and "Try another payment method." Shipped version.

A recovery component was also added directly to the payment screen, so users who chose to retry could still see the alternative without navigating away, reducing the risk that a second payment failure would result in another dead end.

The payment failure dialog with the inline alternative. The recovery option persisted through the retry path, not just the initial failure state.

Outcome

Positive conversion from a moment that previously produced nothing

The solution launched on both app and web. A/B test results confirmed positive net conversion uplift across platforms. The recovery flow was generating bookings that would not have happened otherwise, not just retaining users who would have found their way back regardless.

Drop-off after failed payments decreased significantly. Engagement on the comparison module (image clicks, detail views, scroll depth) confirmed users were reading and acting on the suggestion, not dismissing it.

Results from the A/B testing tool after the feature went fully live. Sensitive numbers blurred for portfolio purposes.

The broader lesson this project surfaced: failure states are where some of the highest-leverage UX improvements live. Most product investment goes into the happy path. But users in failure states are already engaged and motivated. They started a booking, they want to finish it. They need a clear path forward, not a dead end.

Looking back, I would have involved the room selection team in the constraint conversation earlier. When I brought them in at iteration two to discuss deviating from their pattern, they had already formed a strong position. Getting them into the framing conversation before the design work started would have been faster and less adversarial.
