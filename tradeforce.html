Trade Force

Designing a 0→1 product suite to help trade marketing field teams plan, execute and measure their work in real time

TL;DR:

What

As the sole designer at a Brazilian startup, I led end-to-end design for Trade Force - a two-part product (mobile app for field team members + web portal for managers) that replaced paper-based trade marketing workflows with a connected, data-driven solution.

Why

Trade marketing managers had no reliable way to track field teams, and field members wasted hours on manual routing, paper forms and reporting. Existing tools in the market were fragmented, visually inconsistent and lacked smart features.

Main Challenges

Designing two deeply connected products at once, with limited research time, scarce resources and no prior design system - while making the solution accessible to non-tech-savvy users in the field.

How

Through user interviews, affinity mapping, personas, competitor analysis, HMW framing, user flows, sitemaps, wireframes, a shared style guide, high-fidelity prototypes, Lean UX iteration with Hotjar/GA, and later A/B testing once traffic scaled.

Result

Trade Force shipped as a functional MVP, grew into a market-leading solution for trade marketing teams in Brazil, and became the foundation for continuous iteration based on real user feedback and experimentation.

Project background

In the Brazilian trade marketing industry, there was a real opportunity to help companies optimize sales at Points of Sale (POS) through digital tools. The way companies audited promotion, availability and merchandising at the point of sale was far from ideal - field teams had no software to make their work easier, and managers didn't have enough data to make informed decisions.

Trade Force was conceived as a two-part solution to fix exactly that:

  • Trade Force App

    Mobile app for field team members to execute routes, check in/out at POS, fill forms and take pictures.

  • Trade Force Web

    Web portal for trade marketing managers to plan routes, build forms, manage teams and monitor performance through dashboards and reports.

My Role

As the sole designer in the startup, I owned the end-to-end design process for every product surface:

  • Research & Strategy

    Competition analysis, user research, personas, user flows and user stories.

  • Design

    Wireframing, prototyping, UI design and a shared style guide across web and mobile.

  • Validation

    Usability testing, Hotjar surveys, Google Analytics and A/B testing.

  • Delivery

    Front-end development contributions to help ship the MVP and later iterations.

The Challenge

The brief was ambitious: design a solution that would help trade marketing managers create and follow tasks performed by their teams in real time - including tracking routes and product validation at the POS - and visualize relevant data through dashboards with advanced monitoring indicators. At the same time, the product had to help field teams execute their jobs with more efficiency, on mobile, often on low-end devices.

Research — Interviews

During the research phase, I interviewed ten people (five field team members and five managers) to better understand their pain points, motivations and challenges.

  • Interview objectives — Managers

    Understand how managers were currently managing their field teams, how they created itineraries and routes, how they created POS validation forms, and how they gathered and acted on the data coming from the field.

  • Interview objectives — Field team members

    Learn how users felt about each existing tool, how they planned their day, how they executed their daily tasks, and how they reported their work - to create a list of actionable improvements to make their daily routine easier and more enjoyable.

Personas

After consolidating and analyzing the research data, I built two personas that best described the demographics of Trade Force's target market: the trade marketing field team member and the team manager. These personas kept us constantly reminded of the people I was designing for - their needs, wants and pain points.

  • Carla — Retail merchandising promoter

    22, single, completed high school, based in São Mateus, Brazil. Not a tech enthusiast, uses a work-issued device. "I need to provide my manager with all the data gathered from the many POS I visit everyday in a fast, reliable way. I wish I had better route planning as I lose a lot of time commuting."

  • Camila Droghetti — Merchandising coordinator

    37, married with 2 children, post-graduation in Marketing, based in Florianópolis. "I spend too much time inputting data gathered from my field team into spreadsheets, and that makes it harder to take actions based on data. I also wish to track my team's tasks in real time so I can react quickly to any issues."

The two personas that guided the design of Trade Force: Carla (field team member) and Camila (trade marketing manager). [PDF page 4]

Competition analysis

To understand the market Trade Force was entering, I ran an industry benchmark on features, design styles and channels - looking at both immediate and smaller competitors, so I could identify what was already available to customers and how we could do things better.

Patterns between competitors

  • Heavy use of graphical dashboards to display data points
  • Filters for displaying information in reports
  • Similar basic features such as routing, GPS check-in/out, product photography
  • All had a mobile app for field teams and a web dashboard for managers

What could be improved

  • None had consistent design between platforms or a design system in place
  • Most interfaces were confusing, with no clear element hierarchy and missing heuristic rules
  • No smart way to create forms
  • Routing options were very limited and manual
  • It was hard to find specific information inside dashboards
  • Lack of helpful features tied to real user pain points
  • Limited, non-customizable reports
  • Some apps crammed too much info into small screens instead of prioritizing relevance
Snapshot of the competitive landscape review - screenshots and notes from the benchmarked products. [PDF page 5]

Problem statement & How Might We

After gathering the research data, it was time to have a clear description of the issues I needed to address, keeping the focus on users at all times. Together with stakeholders from the company, we ran a "How Might We" session to better define the core problems.

"Trade Marketing managers have a hard time tracking their field team and compiling the data due to the lack of available tools that help their work."
"Field team members waste a lot of time planning and going from one POS to another without a smart route and have to do all their work manually. They also don't have a way to keep track of their work."

From those statements, the HMWs that framed the product were:

  • How might we reduce the amount of time field members spend commuting?
  • How might we enable field team members to be more organized and fill forms faster and more reliably?
  • How might we enable field team members to keep track of their own work?
  • How might we make it easy for TM managers to create routes, activities and tasks for their team?
  • How might we help TM managers track their team's work reliably?
  • How might we help TM managers feel less overwhelmed by data?
The HMW board built with stakeholders, with the problem statements at the top and the generated HMW questions below. [PDF page 6]

User stories & MVP definition

User research, personas and the HMW statements surfaced the core problems. From those, I defined the main issues my design decisions had to solve for the MVP:

  • Managers need to build routes and forms easily

    Create personalized routes for members, build intuitive forms, and keep track of everything.

  • Field members need fast check-in/out

    A mobile flow to check in and out of each POS, with GPS confirmation.

  • Field members need fast, mobile-first execution

    Answer forms and take pictures from their phones, on the go.

  • Managers need data back automatically

    Gathered daily data should be sent back to managers as custom reports and dashboards.

From those, I built user stories and scoped the MVP around six core requirements: Login and register teams, List of points of sale, Map access and route creation, Check-in / Check-out, Form creation and execution, and Reports & Dashboard.

The user stories / MVP requirements table, mapping each requirement to user stories and acceptance criteria. [PDF page 7]

User flow

I mapped out the end-to-end flow for both personas in parallel, so it was clear how the manager's work on the web portal enabled the field member's day on mobile - and how the data flowed back at the end of the day.

  • Field team member

    Login at home → check planned route → go to POS → check-in, fill forms and execute tasks (or justify absence) → check-out → go to next POS → go home when all POS are done.

  • Manager

    Login to web portal → register team members → register / import POS → assign POS to each member based on their address → build daily routes → create forms and tasks → analyze data with reports & dashboards → take action for the next day.

Full user flow diagram showing both field team members and managers on parallel tracks. [PDF page 8]

MVP prototyping — Dashboard

After validating the wireframes with stakeholders and users, and factoring in deadlines and resources, I prototyped the MVP while development started in parallel. As the first users started using the solution, I leaned on a Lean UX approach to collect constant feedback and iterate.

The manager dashboard gave users a high-level view of the main real-time metrics - validated through interviews with clients and stakeholders. Managers could see in real time how the team was performing, how many visits and tasks were completed, how many were pending, and the overall team status. The card-and-column layout allowed more content on screen without sacrificing legibility, and kept the portal responsive across browser sizes.

The main manager dashboard showing real-time metrics, charts and team status. [PDF page 13]

My Team

A section dedicated to everything related to field team members - from registering their data (home address, phone) to accessing each member's personal dashboard and planning their routes.

  • Team list

    A table view with all team members and their main info (role, home address, phone, last login). Users could add new members manually or import a CSV file for bulk registration.

  • Member dashboard

    Clicking a member opened their personal dashboard. I surveyed 10 merchandising managers on which metrics mattered most to evaluate performance - the answers were nearly unanimous, which gave me confidence on what to display. Managers could also check past performance, not only real-time data.

  • Route calendar

    Inspired by Google Calendar, the route calendar let managers set pre-made routes for any day, make them recurring, duplicate, edit or remove them. Routes were shown in a linear/timeline fashion to make the POS sequence easier to understand.

Team list view with table-based layout, filters and add/import actions. [PDF page 14 - top]

Route calendar inspired by Google Calendar, with timeline-style route previews. [PDF page 14 - bottom]

Reports

In this section, managers could extract customized reports about their field team visits. Filtering options let them fine-tune their search, and they could click any row in the table to see the details of the report - for example, the answers for a specific form, at a specific POS, on a specific day. They could also export the data to .xls and .csv, something users repeatedly asked for in interviews because they wanted to manipulate the data in Excel or other tools.

Initially, I proposed showing the report details inside a modal window. But after testing with 5 users and imagining scenarios with bigger forms and more actions, I realized I was cramming too much content into a modal - which is not ideal, and would often require scrolling (a bad practice for modal windows). So I stopped and rethought the approach: a regular page with all that info worked much better and kept consistency with other pages in the app.

Reports list with filters and export actions. [PDF page 15 - left]
The iteration from "report details inside a modal" to "report details as a regular page" - the modal approach broke down as forms grew. [PDF page 15 - right]

Points of Sale

This section held the database of all the points of sale where the client operated, with the information needed to show them on maps, forms and routes. Users could add new POS manually, import a CSV, or use Trade Force's own database of POS based on location and business size, so they didn't have to do the manual work themselves.

The table view displayed the relevant info - address, brand, number of forms, last visits - which proved helpful for users to spot POS that hadn't been visited in a while. Inside each POS dashboard, users found a map, last visits and visit history, and could assign previously created forms to the POS for field team members to execute. A search box with auto-complete and filters made it easy to find forms in long lists.

Points of sale list view, keeping the same table structure as other list pages for consistency. [PDF page 16 - left]
POS detail page with map, last visits, visit history and form assignment panel. [PDF page 16 - right]

Form creator

In this section, users could view, create and edit forms for field team members to answer during their POS visits. First, they selected one of the form types (just for organizational grouping) and a tab - the MVP shipped with types and tabs based on the most common form executions in the market. Users could also set a date range for seasonal research.

The button placement in this screen was intentionally different from the rest of the system: in the first version, users would have to scroll all the way up to save the form. I moved the action menu to a fixed right column so "Add question" and "Save" were always at hand, no matter how long the form got.

For the questions, I used Google Forms as inspiration - users could pick from multiple question types (multiple choice, single choice, plain input, text area, picture, number, SKU), duplicate questions, and reorder them via drag-and-drop. They could also assign products (pre-registered SKUs) to each question, so they could extract bulk information about specific products in the reports. Finally, they could reverse the logic and assign multiple POS to a single form, instead of assigning multiple forms to a POS - useful when a form only applied to a specific store.

Form creator with the fixed right-hand action menu and the question list on the left. [PDF page 17 - top]
Question configuration panel with multiple question types, SKU assignment and drag-and-drop ordering. [PDF page 17 - middle]
Reverse assignment flow: assigning multiple POS to a single form, with address visible to avoid confusion between same-brand stores. [PDF page 17 - bottom]

Route creator

In this section, managers could set up and manage routes for team members. The goal was to make it easy to find and assign POS based on each team member's home address, so they had the shortest commuting time and a logical route to follow every day. From the interviews, I learned that users were very used to Google Maps - so I designed something familiar, with as few steps as possible.

  • 1 — Search and select a member

  • 2 — Create a new route or manage existing ones

    The main action is to add the first POS and continue from there.

  • 3 — Add POS

    Opens a list of POS within a configurable maximum radius from the team member's house, shown both on the map and in the list.

  • 4 — Build the sequence

    As POS are added, their pins turn blue with the visit order inside. The left column shows the route and the time between each stop. Users can reorder POS via drag-and-drop, and the system recalculates the route automatically.

  • 5 — Manage routes

    All created routes for the member are listed, with actions to duplicate, edit and remove. These are the routes used in the calendar for daily planning.

The route creator with the map occupying most of the horizontal space - the same pattern used by Google Maps, which users were already familiar with. [PDF page 18]

Mobile app

The mobile app was where field team members executed the tasks and routes defined by their managers. The login screen was intentionally simple. After login, the app needed a few seconds (sometimes more than 15) to download all the routes and calculations, so I designed a progress indicator to set expectations and reduce the feeling of waiting.

Then users would see the POS visits planned for the day, plus the next four future routes so they could plan ahead. The POS were listed in the order planned by their manager, but users could execute them in any order if circumstances required it. Tapping a POS opened options to check in, justify an absence or see how to get there - with a route suggestion on the map and the option to open the route in Google Maps for more details.

After checking in, users saw the list of tasks and forms to complete, with clear status indicators. Once all forms were completed, they could check out - which prepared all the collected data to be sent back to the manager dashboard. When all POS were done for the day, the user could go home, and the manager already had all the data at their end.

Testing, surveys and feedback

There was no time luxury to test only the prototypes before starting MVP development - so the MVP was launched as designed. Once the product started getting more clients, I instrumented the web app with Hotjar for general usability feedback and surveys, and Google Analytics to understand quantitative metrics (e.g., average time spent on a page or to complete a task). This was mostly true for the web app, as mobile traffic wasn't yet big enough for quick experimentation.

Overall, the result was very satisfactory for a MVP - which reinforced how important it is to have good research, wireframing and stakeholder alignment before jumping into design. The feedback loop helped fine-tune pages, prioritize new features, and validate which aspects of the design were working and which needed iteration.

Sample of Hotjar surveys collected from real users in the web app. [PDF page 20]

Design improvements based on user feedback

The "final" design in this section was the result of dozens of iterations and rounds of user testing - evolving the design language around three concepts: flexibility, clarity and friendliness. These were achieved through a lighter, brighter color palette, a card-based responsive layout, and a friendly, casual tone of voice.

  • Top-level bar with quick shortcuts

    Added to support new features like chat, messaging, alerts and quick info access.

  • Collapsible sidebar

    Let users maximize visible content when they needed more room.

  • Table sorters

    Improved usability of the many table views in the product.

  • Collapsible filters

    Reduced information overload and avoided unnecessary scrolling.

  • Hover tooltips and info panels

    Surfaced context-specific information without overloading the screen.

  • Better sliders

    Showed clear value ranges for POS routing and allowed users to type the value directly if preferred.

Examples of the redesigned pages after iterating with Hotjar and user testing feedback. [PDF page 21]

A/B testing

For the mobile app, once the company grew and acquired more clients, we had enough traffic to run A/B tests on specific improvements. A few examples:

  • Hypothesis 1 — Linear POS list

    "According to our data and research, ~23% of users do not execute POS in the ideal order suggested by the manager. We believe that by displaying the POS list in a timeline/linear fashion, users will have a more structured understanding of the execution order. We will understand success when we see an increase of routes being executed in the perfect order."

    Metrics: # of routes executed in the suggested order, # of user inbound.
    Results: Increase in routes executed in suggested order was not significant. +4.3% in inbound. Not full-on.

  • Hypothesis 2 — More readable badges

    "According to user testing, 23% of users found the badges hard to read. As we add more form types, scrolling happens and some form names are very long, breaking the design in some cases. We aim to make the content more readable and avoid repeated information, measured with a non-inferiority test."

    Metrics & criteria: increase in time on page ≤10%, % of user inbound ≤1%.
    Results: Non-inferiority criteria met. Time on page decreased by 8%. Easier to add more content without breaking the design. Follow-up research confirmed users clearly understood the number as pending forms. FULL-ON.

  • Hypothesis 3 — Collapsible form questions

    "According to user feedback, forms can be very long and users find it hard to keep track of every question. We aim to solve this by making questions collapsible/expandable and showing the answer inline. We will measure average time spent in completing forms, amount of scrolling, and how many forms are submitted with unanswered questions."

    Metrics: average time per form section, average completion time, form submissions with unanswered questions.
    Results: Average time per section decreased by ~8%. Form submissions with unanswered questions decreased by 21%. Users said the new version made them feel more secure about submitting the right answers and gave them a better overview. FULL-ON.

Final Thoughts

Trade Force was a defining project in my career - I was the sole designer, which meant owning everything from research and strategy to UI, front-end development and post-launch experimentation. It taught me how to make confident decisions under constraint, how to lean on research to earn stakeholder trust, and how to build products that are as much about the data flowing between them as they are about the screens users see.

Most importantly, it reinforced how valuable it is to design for real users with real jobs - in this case, field team members driving across cities in traffic, and managers drowning in spreadsheets. Every decision I made came back to making their day a little easier, and that's still the lens I design through today.
